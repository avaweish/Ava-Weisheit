What a 59% Accurate Model Taught Me About Good Data Science

Data Science Machine Learning

When I built a decision tree to classify beer styles by flavor attributes, it landed at 59% accuracy. My first instinct was to see that as a problem to fix. My second, better instinct was to ask what that number was actually telling me.

Accuracy isn't the whole story

A 59% accurate model sounds mediocre in isolation. But context changes everything: this was a multi-class problem across a wide range of beer styles, several of which share overlapping flavor profiles by design. A model that separates IPA and Sour beers with precision scores above 0.65 while struggling on Lambic and Porter isn't failing randomly — it's revealing something true about how those styles actually taste.

The confusion matrix is the real deliverable

Early on, I was tempted to treat the accuracy score as the headline result. The confusion matrix ended up being more useful. Strong diagonal values for easily separable styles, paired with meaningful off-diagonal activity for overlapping ones, told a more honest story than a single summary statistic ever could: sensory attributes alone can't fully capture what distinguishes some beer categories.

Why this generalizes

This is a lesson that shows up constantly outside of beer classification. A model's job isn't to hit an arbitrary accuracy threshold — it's to be honest about what the data can and can't explain. Sometimes an "imperfect" model that surfaces where a domain is genuinely ambiguous is more valuable than an inflated one that's overfit to noise.

Where I'd take it next

Additional features beyond taste attributes — brewing method, ABV, regional style conventions — would likely improve separability for the harder categories. But I'd rather report a 59% model that tells the truth about its limits than a 95% model that's quietly memorized the training set.
