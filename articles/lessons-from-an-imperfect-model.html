What a 59% Accurate Model Taught Me About Good Data Science

Data Science Machine Learning

I built a decision tree to classify beer styles by their flavor attributes - bitterness, sweetness, that kind of thing - and it came out at 59% accuracy. My gut reaction was "okay, that needs fixing." My better instinct, once I actually sat with it for a bit, was to ask what that number was actually telling me instead of just trying to inflate it.

Accuracy isn't the whole story

59% sounds mediocre on its own, I get it. But this was a multi-class problem across a lot of beer styles, and several of them share overlapping flavor profiles on purpose - that's kind of the point of some of these styles. The model separated IPAs and Sours pretty well, with precision above 0.65 for both, but struggled with Lambic and Porter. That's not the model being lazy. That's the model picking up on something real: those styles genuinely taste more alike than, say, an IPA and a Porter do.

The confusion matrix told me more than the accuracy score did

Early on I kept fixating on the accuracy number like it was the whole point. The confusion matrix ended up being way more useful. Strong values down the diagonal for the styles that are easy to tell apart, plus a decent amount of overlap for the styles that aren't - that's a more honest picture than one summary number could ever give me. It basically said "flavor alone can't fully separate every beer category, and that's fine."

Why I think this matters beyond beer

This is a pattern I now notice all over the place, not just in this one project. A model's job isn't to hit some arbitrary accuracy target - it's to be honest about what the data can and can't explain. An "imperfect" model that's upfront about where a domain gets genuinely ambiguous is more useful to me than an inflated one that quietly memorized noise in the training set.

What I'd try next

If I revisit this, I'd add features beyond taste - brewing method, ABV, regional style conventions - to help with the harder-to-separate categories. But honestly, I'd rather hand in a 59% model that's honest about its limits than a 95% one that's secretly just memorized the answers.
