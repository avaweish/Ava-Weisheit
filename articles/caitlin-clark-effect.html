Why the Caitlin Clark Effect Is Bigger Than One Player

Sports Analytics Data Science

When Caitlin Clark entered the WNBA, attendance numbers moved — visibly, immediately, and in ways that made national headlines. But the more time I've spent digging into the attendance and social trend data for my ongoing research project, the more I've realized the "Clark effect" story is doing a lot of narrative work that the numbers only partially support.

The number everyone quotes

League-wide attendance rose sharply during Clark's rookie season, and games she played in consistently drew larger crowds, including on the road. That part of the story holds up in the data I've collected. What's less discussed is how much of that lift was concentrated in a handful of markets and games, rather than a uniform league-wide bump.

What the regression actually shows

Running linear regression against attendance and Google Trends data, Clark's presence is a statistically significant predictor of attendance — but it's not the only one. Team win percentage, rivalry games, and season timing all carry real weight too. Isolating her individual effect means controlling for those variables, which is exactly the kind of work that's easy to skip when you're writing a headline instead of a model.

Why this matters beyond basketball

This is a pattern I keep running into in sports analytics broadly: a single compelling storyline (a star player, a big trade, a viral moment) becomes the explanation for a trend that's actually driven by several overlapping factors. Good analysis doesn't mean dismissing the storyline — Clark's impact is real — it means being precise about how much of the effect belongs to her versus everything happening around her.

What's next
